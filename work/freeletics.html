Freeletics

Building a segmentation model that's still the foundation, years after shipping

Context

Freeletics segmented users by fitness level only — beginner, intermediate, advanced. It was a proxy for physical ability, not for why someone trains, which meant features couldn't be designed with any real understanding of what would actually motivate someone to keep going.

My role & approach

Product management asked me to explore motivation psychology, specifically self-determination theory (SDT). I spent the first weeks reading into the field properly — SDT, intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, self-efficacy, autonomy, competence, relatedness — before recommending we base a proper market segmentation on it. Working with an external research agency, I delivered six segments built on SDT combined with personality and training behaviour, spanning a spectrum from amotivated through to intrinsically motivated.

To bring the rest of the business along, I ran a series of short "research bites" — one motivation concept at a time, explained simply, each paired with a clear "opportunity for Freeletics" so the theory always connected back to something actionable.

The insight

Motivation type is a far more stable segmentation axis than behaviour or fitness level, because how someone is motivated tends to hold steady even as their behaviour changes over time. That durability is what makes the framework worth investing in — it doesn't need re-doing every time usage patterns shift.

What changed

The six segments became the basis for how product and design thought about users going forward, replacing the old beginner/intermediate/advanced split as the working mental model.

Outcome — I left Freeletics three and a half years ago. The segmentation is still in use today — since updated and built on, but with my original framework as its foundation.

Reflection

The "research bites" format ended up mattering as much as the research itself. A senior researcher's real leverage often isn't just doing the analysis correctly — it's translating academic theory into something a whole product organisation can pick up and use without reading the white papers themselves.
